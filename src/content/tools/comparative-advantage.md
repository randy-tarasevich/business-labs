## How I use it

**International Business, early in the course.** 25–30 minutes. The idea is counterintuitive, so give it time.

1. **Predict:** Before loading anything: "If one country is better at making everything, should it trade?" Most students say no.
2. **Test:** Start with the two islands. Students find the opportunity costs (the table does it for them as a check), click **Specialize**, then negotiate a trade in pairs, one student per island. Each island must end up with at least as much of both goods.
3. **Explain:** Load Portugal and England (David Ricardo's original 1817 example). Portugal is better at both goods, and both still win. Students write one sentence on why.

**Controls:** the slider sets what share of each country's workers make the first good. "Set as before trade" saves the current mix as the starting point for comparison. "Undo trade" puts production back and clears the trade. Students can type in any maximums to build their own scenarios.

The model uses straight-line production possibilities (constant opportunity cost), which is the standard classroom version.

## Answer notes

- **Two islands:** opportunity cost of 1 fish is 4 coconuts on Palm Island and 1 coconut on Reef Island. Reef specializes in fish (20), Palm in coconuts (40). World output goes from 15 fish and 30 coconuts to 20 and 40. A trade that works: Palm sends 15 coconuts for 7 fish. Palm ends with 7 fish, 25 coconuts (was 5 and 20); Reef with 13 fish, 15 coconuts (was 10 and 10). Any price between 1 and 4 coconuts per fish can work.
- **Portugal and England:** before trade Portugal has 54 wine and 32 cloth; England has 30 and 36. Specialize, then Portugal sends 33 wine for 34 cloth: Portugal ends with 57 and 34, England with 33 and 38. The price has to fall between 0.89 and 1.2 cloth per wine.
- **Same costs:** both countries give up 0.5 tractors per unit of corn. No comparative advantage, so specializing doesn't grow the world total, and no trade makes both better off.

## Predict first

Two prediction cards sit above the challenges. Students pick Goes up, Goes down, or Stays the same; the tool makes the change on screen and shows before and after.

- **Palm gets twice as good at everything** (10 fish or 40 coconuts to 20 or 80): the coconuts Palm gives up per fish **stay the same** at 4. Opportunity cost is a ratio, so being better at both doesn't change it. Follow-up: does Palm now have a comparative advantage in fish? (No. It has the absolute advantage in both, but Reef still gives up less per fish.)
- **Portugal and England specialize** (all wine, all cloth): world wine **goes up**, 84 to 90, and cloth goes from 68 to 72. Same workers, more stuff. Follow-up: who gets the extra? (Whoever the trade gives it to. That's what the trade boxes are for.)

## Challenges

Three challenge cards sit under the tool. Each Start button loads its scenario and makes a link you can post. Sample solutions (there are others):

- **Both islands win** (what each island can make stays the same): Palm makes all coconuts (share 0), Reef makes all fish (share 100). Palm sends 15 coconuts and gets 7 fish back: Palm ends with 7 fish and 25 coconuts (+2, +5), Reef with 13 fish and 15 coconuts (+3, +5). Any price between 1 and 4 coconuts per fish can work. Ask why the price has to land in that range.
- **More of everything** (Portugal and England): Portugal all wine, England all cloth gives 90 wine and 72 cloth, up from 84 and 68. Partial moves in the same direction also work. Debrief: Portugal is better at both. Why does it still pay to let England make cloth?
- **Give trade a reason** (Northland locked, Southland still at most 60 corn): raise Southland's max tractors to 60, then Northland all corn and Southland all tractors: 100 corn and 60 tractors, up from 80 and 55. The key is that opportunity costs must differ before specializing helps.

## Common misconceptions

- Students confuse absolute advantage (who makes more) with comparative advantage (who gives up less). The table shows both side by side.
- Students think the stronger country "wins" trade and the weaker one loses. Ricardo's example is the counterexample.
- Students propose trades outside the range of the two opportunity costs. One side always ends up worse off; that's a good discussion about what a fair price is.
- Students think trade only means buying what you can't make. These countries can make both goods; they trade because it's cheaper.
