## How I use it

**Level: Advanced Accounting.** 15–20 minutes, after students have done one straight-line schedule by hand (the paper problem below works for that).

1. **Predict:** Students sketch what they think the book value line looks like for each method before loading the food truck.
2. **Test:** Compare their sketches to the chart, then work the "Try it" questions. The schedule tabs show every year in detail, in cents, so students can check homework.
3. **Explain:** Each pair writes one sentence on why a business might pick double-declining for taxes but straight-line for the report it shows the bank.

The tool calculates full years only (no partial first year). Double-declining never drops below salvage, and its final year is whatever brings book value down to salvage. If your textbook switches to straight-line partway through instead, say so; the totals still match.

## Paper first

10–15 minutes, the day before or at the start of class. The tool shows every method instantly, so students need to have built at least one schedule themselves to know what it's doing.

**Put this on the board:** A bakery buys a delivery van for $35,000. It expects to use it for 5 years and sell it for $5,000 at the end.

1. Using straight-line, how much depreciation each year?
2. Make a 5-year schedule: depreciation, accumulated depreciation, and book value at the end of each year.
3. Using double-declining balance, what is the rate? Find depreciation for years 1 and 2.

**Answers:** (1) ($35,000 − $5,000) ÷ 5 = $6,000 a year. (2) Book value: $29,000, $23,000, $17,000, $11,000, $5,000. Accumulated: $6,000, $12,000, $18,000, $24,000, $30,000. (3) Rate is 2 ÷ 5 = 40%. Year 1: $35,000 × 40% = $14,000. Year 2: ($35,000 − $14,000) × 40% = $8,400. The most common mistake is applying 40% to $30,000 (cost minus salvage) instead of book value.

**Then the tool:** load Delivery van and open the schedule tabs to check. What the tool adds: the rest of the double-declining schedule (year 4 stops at salvage, which is fiddly by hand), sum-of-years without the arithmetic, and the chart comparing all three on one screen.

## Answer notes

**Food truck** ($60,000 cost, $6,000 salvage, 8 years; $54,000 to depreciate):
- Straight-line: $6,750 every year.
- Double-declining: rate 25%. Year 1 $15,000, year 2 $11,250, year 3 $8,437.50. Year 8 is $2,009.03 to land on $6,000.
- Sum-of-years: digits total 36. Year 1 = $54,000 × 8/36 = $12,000; year 8 = $1,500.

**Laptop** ($1,500, $100 salvage, 3 years):
- Straight-line: $466.67 per year.
- Double-declining: $1,000, then $333.33, then $66.67.
- Sum-of-years: $700, $466.67, $233.33.

## Predict first

Two prediction cards sit above the challenges. Students pick Goes up, Goes down, or Stays the same; the tool makes the change on screen and shows before and after. Works well projected: take a show of hands, click the class favorite, then discuss.

- **Straight-line to double-declining** (food truck): total depreciation **stays the same** at $54,000. Every method spreads cost minus salvage; faster methods just move expense into earlier years. Targets the "faster means more expense" misconception.
- **Double-declining to sum-of-years** (delivery van): Year 1 depreciation **goes down**, $14,000 to $10,000. Both are accelerated, but double-declining front-loads harder. Follow-up: when does sum-of-years pass double-declining? (Have students compare Year 3.)

## Challenges

Three challenge cards sit under the tool. Each Start button loads its scenario and makes a link you can post. Sample solutions:

- **Biggest first-year write-off** (delivery van): double-declining. Year 1 is $14,000 vs. $10,000 sum-of-years and $6,000 straight-line. Follow up: does it write off more in total? (No, all three total $30,000.)
- **$400 a year** (laptop, straight-line, life 2 or more): life 3 with salvage $300, or life 2 with salvage $700. Ask which is more believable for a laptop.
- **Write it off faster** (pizza oven, sum-of-years, only change life): life 6 or shorter. Life 6 gives 11,000 × 6 ÷ 21 = $3,142.86. Shorter life means bigger early expense.

## Common misconceptions

- Students think depreciation is cash going out. The cash left when the asset was bought; depreciation just spreads the cost.
- Students depreciate the full cost. Salvage value is never depreciated.
- Students apply the double-declining rate to cost minus salvage. It applies to **book value**, and salvage only acts as a floor.
- Students think faster methods mean more total expense. Every method totals cost minus salvage.
