## How I use it

**Level: Accounting 1**, after students can journalize. Also works as a review at the start of Advanced Accounting. 20 minutes.

1. **Predict:** Project the bakery with the statements covered. Students build the income statement on paper from the transaction list.
2. **Test:** Uncover and compare. Then pairs work the "Try it" questions, turning transactions on and off.
3. **Explain:** Each student writes two sentences tracing one number from the income statement to the balance sheet.

Pointing at (or tabbing to) a transaction highlights every line it touches across all three statements. Students can also change amounts, which makes it easy to build "what if" questions of your own.

This tool skips the journal and ledger on purpose. It's about how the statements connect, not about posting.

## Answer notes

**Sweet Spot Bakery, March:** revenue $4,300, expenses $3,350, net income $950. Capital goes from $7,200 to $8,350 ($7,200 + $1,000 investment + $950 − $800 withdrawal). Total assets $13,450 = liabilities $5,100 + capital $8,350.

- Uncheck the withdrawal: ending capital becomes $9,150. Net income stays $950.
- Rent above $1,950 creates a net loss (rent of $2,000 gives a $50 loss).

**Green Edge Lawn Care, June:** revenue $3,500, expenses $1,670, net income $1,830. Capital goes from $6,350 to $7,180. Total assets $7,630 = liabilities $450 + capital $7,180.

## Predict first

Two prediction cards sit above the challenges. Students pick Goes up, Goes down, or Stays the same; the tool makes the change on screen and shows before and after.

- **Bigger withdrawal** (bakery, owner withdraws $1,600 instead of $800): net income **stays the same** at $950. A withdrawal isn't an expense; it shows up on the statement of owner's equity, not the income statement. Follow-up: where does the extra $800 show up? (Cash and ending capital both drop by $800.)
- **More supplies used** (bakery, $700 to $1,000 used up, no cash spent): ending capital **goes down**, $8,350 to $8,050. More supplies expense means less net income, which flows into capital. Follow-up: did cash change? (No. Supplies on the balance sheet dropped instead.)

## Challenges

Three challenge cards sit under the tool. Each Start button loads its scenario and makes a link you can post. Sample solutions (there are others):

- **Cash is not profit** (bakery, net income stays $950): the owner invests $2,050 instead of $1,000, or withdraws nothing and pays the supplier only $250, or skips the loan payment and withdraws less. None of these are revenue or expenses. Debrief: list three transactions that change cash but not profit.
- **Profit is not cash** (lawn care, ending cash stays $3,030 or less): bill $1,570 of landscaping on account instead of $900 (net income $2,500, cash unchanged). Cutting cash expenses also raises profit, but it raises cash too, so it breaks the rule.
- **Pay the owner more** (bakery, withdrawal $1,500, investment stays $1,000): net income has to rise by $700. Cash sales of $3,900, or wages of $700, both work. Point out the chain: income statement, then owner's equity, then balance sheet.

## Common misconceptions

- Students put withdrawals on the income statement. They only appear on the statement of owner's equity.
- Students list loan payments as expenses. Repaying the principal only lowers cash and the loan balance.
- "Supplies expense" confuses students: buying supplies adds an asset; **using** them is the expense.
- Students try to build the balance sheet first. Without ending capital from statement 2, it can't balance.
