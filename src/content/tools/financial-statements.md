## How I use it

**Level: Accounting 1**, after students can journalize. Also works as a review at the start of Advanced Accounting. 20 minutes.

1. **Predict:** Project the bakery with the statements covered. Students build the income statement on paper from the transaction list.
2. **Test:** Uncover and compare. Then pairs work the "Try it" questions, turning transactions on and off.
3. **Explain:** Each student writes two sentences tracing one number from the income statement to the balance sheet.

Pointing at (or tabbing to) a transaction highlights every line it touches across all three statements. Students can also change amounts, which makes it easy to build "what if" questions of your own.

This tool skips the journal and ledger on purpose. It's about how the statements connect, not about posting.

## Answer notes

**Sweet Spot Bakery, March:** revenue $4,300, expenses $3,350, net income $950. Capital goes from $7,200 to $8,350 ($7,200 + $1,000 investment + $950 − $800 withdrawal). Total assets $13,450 = liabilities $5,100 + capital $8,350.

- Uncheck the withdrawal: ending capital becomes $9,150. Net income stays $950.
- Rent above $1,950 creates a net loss (rent of $2,000 gives a $50 loss).

**Green Edge Lawn Care, June:** revenue $3,500, expenses $1,670, net income $1,830. Capital goes from $6,350 to $7,180. Total assets $7,630 = liabilities $450 + capital $7,180.

## Common misconceptions

- Students put withdrawals on the income statement. They only appear on the statement of owner's equity.
- Students list loan payments as expenses. Repaying the principal only lowers cash and the loan balance.
- "Supplies expense" confuses students: buying supplies adds an asset; **using** them is the expense.
- Students try to build the balance sheet first. Without ending capital from statement 2, it can't balance.
