## How I use it

**Social Media Marketing, influencer unit.** 20–25 minutes. Comes right after Engagement Rate Lab and before Marketing Funnel.

1. **Predict:** Load the phone cases and project only the Followers and Fee rows (cover the rest). Students vote: which creator should the brand hire? Most pick the big creator.
2. **Test:** Uncover the table and read the chart together. Then pairs work the "Try it" questions, switching campaigns as they go.
3. **Explain:** Each pair writes a one-paragraph pitch to the brand's marketing manager: which creator to hire, the number that proves it, and one risk.

The three campaigns are built to break any single rule. Phone cases says "smaller is better." Sneakers says "check for fake followers." The video game says "a big creator with a real audience can win." Students should end up with a process (find real fans, then cost per real fan, then profit), not a rule.

**Extension:** with the phone cases, the budget is the big creator's $6,000 fee. How many small creators could the brand hire instead, and what would they make together? (75 small creators, about $4,875 in profit, if each one's fans are different people.) Then ask why that might not work as well in real life: managing 75 creators is a lot of work, and their audiences overlap.

**Disclosure:** the FTC note at the bottom of "The math" is worth two minutes. Ask students to find a paid post in their own feed and point to how it's labeled.

## Predict first

Two prediction cards sit above the challenges. Students pick Goes up, Goes down, or Stays the same; the tool makes the change on screen and shows before and after.

- **Higher price** (phone cases, $30 to $40 average order, big creator): customers **stay the same**, about 80.6. In this model, price changes how much each customer spends, not how many fans click and buy. Follow-up: is that realistic? (Not entirely; a higher price would likely lose some buyers. Good discussion.)
- **Double the engagement, double the fee** (phone cases, mid creator, 4% to 8% and $350 to $700): profit **goes up**, about $167 to $333. Twice the real fans doubles the sales, and the fee was the smaller piece. Follow-up: what's the most she could charge for 8% and still break even? (About $1,033.)

## Challenges

Three challenge cards sit under the tool. Each Start button loads its scenario and makes a link you can post. Sample solutions (there are others):

- **Negotiate the fee** (phone cases, big creator): the post brings in about $1,572 of profit before the fee, so a fee of $1,572 or less breaks even ($1,500 works). She asked for $6,000. Ask: would she take that? What does this tell you about follower counts?
- **How fake is too fake?** (sneaker shop, mid creator, only the fake share changes): 35% or less breaks even (about $7 profit at 35%, a $3 loss at 36%). Debrief: how could a brand check for fake followers before paying?
- **Grow the small creator** (phone cases, small creator, fee and rates locked): about 32,000 followers earns $500 (31,000 is about $483). Four times her audience, and still a fraction of the big creator's fee.

## Common misconceptions

- **"More followers means more sales."** Sales come from real people who pay attention. The big phone-case creator has 800,000 followers but only 6,720 real fans who engage.
- **"The cheapest cost per fan is always the best deal."** Cost per fan measures efficiency, not size. A tiny creator can be efficient and still bring in too few customers to matter.
- **"Engagement is the same as buying."** Even real fans mostly scroll on. In these campaigns, about 1 in 100 real fans or fewer becomes a customer.
- **"Fake followers are harmless, they're just extra."** The brand pays for them. The fee is set by the follower count, so every fake follower raises the price of every real one.
