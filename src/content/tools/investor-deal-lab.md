## How I use it

A 15–20 minute **predict, test, explain** activity. Students know the format from TV pitch shows, so start there: "She asked for $50,000 for 10%. What does she think her company is worth?"

1. **Predict:** Before opening the tool, students write the valuation for the smoothie truck ask and the counteroffer.
2. **Test:** Load the preset and check. Then pairs work the "Try it" questions.
3. **Explain:** Each pair writes one sentence on why giving up more equity can still be the better deal.

**Mock pitch extension:** half the class are founders, half are investors. Founders set an ask for their own business idea; investors counter. Both sides have to defend their valuation using the "Valuation ÷ yearly sales" row.

## Answer notes

- **Smoothie truck:** the ask values the company at $500,000 ($50,000 ÷ 10%). The counteroffer values it at $200,000 ($50,000 ÷ 25%), 60% less. At a $1 million sale the founder gets $900,000 vs. $750,000.
- **Question 2:** $100,000 for 20% keeps the $500,000 valuation.
- **Gaming headset:** the ask is $300,000 for 2%, a $15 million valuation, about 17 times sales. If the company sells for $10 million, the investor gets $200,000 back on $300,000 (a 0.7× return, a loss). The counteroffer ($300,000 for 10%) is $3 million, about 3.3 times sales.
- **Dog treat bakery:** a modest ask ($125,000, about 3.1 times sales). Good contrast with the headset brand.
- **Question 4:** 90% of $1 million is $900,000. 75% of $2 million is $1.5 million. The bigger pie wins by $600,000.

## Common misconceptions

- Students think the valuation is the money the founder gets. The investment goes into the company, not the founder's pocket.
- Students think keeping more percent is always better. Question 4 is the counterexample.
- Students treat the valuation as a fact. It's a negotiation: two people looking at the same business and disagreeing on what it's worth.
- "Value before the money" (pre-money) confuses students. It's the company's worth before the investor's cash arrives. Introduce the term only after the main idea lands.

The tool assumes the founder owns 100% before the deal and that no other investors join later. Real deals add complications like royalties, loans, and future rounds that shrink everyone's slice.
