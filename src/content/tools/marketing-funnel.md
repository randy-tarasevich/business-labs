## How I use it

**Marketing, promotion unit.** 15–20 minutes.

1. **Predict:** Show only the bakery's inputs. Students guess how many customers $500 of ads will bring in. Most guess far too high.
2. **Test:** Load the preset and walk down the funnel together. Then pairs work the "Try it" questions.
3. **Explain:** Each pair writes a short "memo to the owner": keep running the ad, change it, or stop. They have to cite one number.

**Extension:** give each group a different lever (click rate, conversion rate, average order, cost per view) and a goal of $200 profit. Groups report what change their lever needed and whether it's realistic.

The funnel shape is drawn for readability, not to scale. Real drop-offs are steep: the bakery goes from 62,500 views to 30 customers.

## Answer notes

| Preset | Views | Clicks | Customers | Revenue | Profit after ads | Sales per $1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bakery | 62,500 | 750 | 30 | $840 | $4 | 1.68× |
| Tutoring | 20,000 | 800 | 24 | $7,200 | $4,240 | 9.00× |
| T-shirts | 200,000 | 1,600 | 32 | $800 | −$640 | 0.80× |

- T-shirt break-even conversion rate: about 5.6% (needs about 89 customers).
- The bakery's ads bring in $1.68 for every $1, yet only make $4. Revenue isn't profit: 40% of each sale goes to the product.

## Predict first

Two prediction cards sit above the challenges. Students pick Goes up, Goes down, or Stays the same; the tool makes the change on screen and shows before and after. Works well projected: take a show of hands, click the class favorite, then discuss.

- **Twice the clicks** (T-shirt video ads, 0.8% to 1.6%): profit after ads **goes up**, from a $640 loss to a $280 loss. Many students expect doubling clicks to make the campaign profitable. Still a loss: doubling one step of the funnel isn't enough when the rest is weak. Follow-up: what else would have to change?
- **Bigger orders** (bakery, average order $28 to $35): cost per customer **stays the same** at $16.67. It's budget divided by customers, and a bigger order doesn't bring in more customers. Profit does go up. Good bridge to why businesses chase bigger orders from the customers they already paid for.

## Challenges

Three challenge cards sit under the tool. Each Start button loads its scenario and makes a link you can post. Sample solutions (there are others):

- **Make the ads pay** (bakery, $250 profit, budget $500 or less, cost per 1,000 views and margin locked): click rate 1.8% gives $256. Conversion rate 6% or average order $42 also work. Ask which one a bakery can actually change (better photos, a bundle deal).
- **Cheaper customers** (T-shirts, cost per customer under $20, budget $1,000): conversion rate 3.2% gives 51 customers at $19.53 each, or click rate 1.3%. Average order doesn't change cost per customer, which is a useful surprise.
- **Scale up** (tutor, budget $1,600, $70 per 1,000 views, sales per $1 back to 6×): conversion rate 3.6% gives 6.17×, or click rate 4.7%. Connects to the misconception that doubling budget doubles results.

## Common misconceptions

- Students judge an ad by views. Views are cheap; customers are what matter. Compare cost per customer.
- Students think sales per $1 above 1.0 means profit. With a 60% margin, the bakery needs about 1.67× just to break even.
- Students assume doubling the budget doubles results. In practice, extra spending reaches less interested people.
- Conversion rate and click rate get mixed up. Click rate is viewers who click; conversion rate is clickers who buy.
