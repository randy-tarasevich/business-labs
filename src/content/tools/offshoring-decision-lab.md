## How I use it

**International Business, global production unit.** 20–25 minutes. Comes after Currency and Trade Lab (students already know what tariffs and exchange rates do) and before Market Entry Lab.

1. **Predict:** Show only the two wages for each product: $18 vs. $2.50 for T-shirts, $24 vs. $5 for furniture, $30 vs. $6 for headphones. Students predict where each product should be made. Most say "overseas" for all three.
2. **Test:** Load each product and compare. Then pairs work the "Try it" questions.
3. **Explain:** Each pair writes a short memo to a CEO: where to make the headphones, the two numbers that decided it, and one risk the tool doesn't show.

The big idea is that wage is only one line in the cost. T-shirts are mostly labor, so low wages win easily. Furniture is mostly materials and shipping, so low wages don't matter much. Headphones sit in the middle, which is why a tariff or a currency change can flip the decision. This connects to current news about tariffs and companies moving production ("reshoring").

**Extension:** have students change only "Hours to make one" at the home factory to show what automation does. For T-shirts, home would have to make a shirt in about 0.15 hours (9 minutes) to tie with overseas.

## Answer notes

| Product | Home per good unit | Overseas per good unit | Overseas saves | Per year | Tariff that ties |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T-shirts | $12.55 | $6.08 | $6.47 | $647,207 | about 152% |
| Furniture | $170.41 | $183.51 | −$13.10 | −$65,512 | about 11% |
| Headphones | $53.54 | $63.30 | −$9.76 | −$488,181 | about 5% |

Overseas breakdowns:

| Product | Labor | Materials | Shipping | Tariff | Landed | Defect rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T-shirts | $2.00 | $2.50 | $0.60 | $0.67 | $5.77 | 5% |
| Furniture | $20.00 | $70.00 | $60.00 | $22.50 | $172.50 | 6% |
| Headphones (10% currency change) | $7.92 | $39.60 | $2.00 | $11.88 | $61.40 | 3% |

- Furniture (Try it #3): overseas shipping of about $47.68 per unit ties with home.
- Headphones (Try it #4): with no tariff, overseas costs $51.05 (cheaper than home's $53.54). With the tariff but no currency change, overseas costs $57.73. With neither, $46.60.
- The "tariff that ties" for headphones is lower (5%) than students expect because the 10% currency change already raised overseas costs.
- The tariff is charged on the factory cost (labor plus materials), not on shipping. Real tariff rules are more complicated; this keeps the math readable.

## Predict first

Two prediction cards sit above the challenges. Students pick Goes up, Goes down, or Stays the same; the tool makes the change on screen and shows before and after.

- **Make twice as many shirts** (T-shirts, 100,000 to 200,000 a year): the overseas cost per good shirt **stays the same** at $6.08. In this tool each shirt costs the same no matter how many you make; what doubles is the total savings. Follow-up: in real life, why might the cost per shirt drop with more volume? (Bulk materials, spreading fixed costs.)
- **Fewer defects overseas** (furniture, 6% to 2%): the overseas cost per good piece **goes down**, about $183.51 to $176.02. Every bad piece has to be paid for and made again. Follow-up: is that enough to beat making it at home? (No. Home is still about $170.41.)

## Challenges

Three challenge cards sit under the tool. Each Start button loads its scenario and makes a link you can post. Sample solutions (there are others):

- **The tariff that brings it home** (T-shirts, factory and currency locked): about 152% or more. The tool's break-even tariff is about 151.6%. Point out how big a tariff it takes when labor is that much cheaper.
- **A better factory** (furniture, overseas wage, materials, shipping, and tariff locked): cut hours to 3 and defects to 2% (about $169.60 vs. $170.41 at home), or cut hours to 2 with the same 6% defects. Shipping a couch across an ocean is the real problem.
- **Currency swing** (headphones, factories and tariff locked): a currency change of −8% or lower (about $53.28 overseas vs. $53.54 at home). It started at +10%, so that's an 18-point swing. Debrief: why do companies worry about exchange rates they can't control?

## Common misconceptions

- **"Lower wages always mean lower costs."** Overseas workers may take more hours per unit, and wages are only part of the cost. Furniture shows shipping and tariffs can outweigh a wage gap of almost 5 to 1.
- **"Tariffs are paid by the other country."** The importing company pays the tariff at the border. In this tool it shows up as a cost to the overseas option, paid by the company that brings the goods in.
- **"A stronger foreign currency helps the company."** The company pays the factory in that currency, so a stronger foreign currency makes everything it buys there cost more dollars.
- **"Defects are just a quality problem."** Every bad unit has to be made again, so the defect rate raises the cost of every good unit.
