## How I use it

**Marketing, after the Product Life Cycle Lab and before pricing.** Also opens the Entrepreneurship sequence: is there a group of customers no one is serving? 20–30 minutes.

1. **Predict:** Project Pizza in town. Before moving anything, students write down where they would open a new pizza place and why. Most pick a spot right next to the biggest bubble (Families).
2. **Test:** Students try their spot, then work the Try it questions in pairs. Each one is a different trap: copying a crowded spot (#1, #3) and chasing an empty corner (#2).
3. **Explain:** Exit ticket: "Name the customer group you would target in Running shoes and explain why that position beats the alternatives." That's the target market standard (MP:005) in one sentence.

**Paper first (optional):** hand out a blank grid with the pizza brands and bubbles drawn on it. Students mark their spot and predict how many customers they'd win before using the tool to check.

Every market starts your brand in the bottom-right corner (expensive and basic) on purpose. It wins nothing, so the first move is always an improvement.

## Answer notes

How many customers your brand wins at a few spots (rounded):

| Market | Best spot: the gap | Copy the biggest group | Join the premium crowd | Cheap-but-premium corner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pizza in town (1,250 customers) | **350** at Good pizza, fair price (4.5, 8) | 236 at Families (4, 4.5), next to Planet Pie | 132 at Date night (8, 8.5) | (2, 9): wins the gap group, but flagged as hard to afford |
| Snack bars (1,200) | **300** at Healthy on a budget (3.5, 7.5) | 199 at After-school treat (2.5, 2.5) | 127 at Gym regulars (8, 8) | 0 at (1, 9.5): no group in reach |
| Running shoes (1,000) | **400** at New runners (5, 6.5) | 200 at Everyday walkers (2.5, 3) | 83 at Serious racers (8.5, 8.5), split four ways | 0 at (3, 9) |

Students sometimes squeeze out a few more customers by sitting between the gap and a second group (pizza at (5, 6.5) wins about 368). That's a fine discussion: is a position that serves two groups halfway really better than owning one?

**The model, if students ask.** Customers only consider brands within 3 units of what they want (the dashed circle). They split among those brands, and closer brands get more. Brands that already exist get 3 times the pull of a new one, because they have regulars. The "hard to afford" warning appears when the up-down feature is 5 or more above price: this much quality (or healthiness, or performance) at that price usually costs more to make than it sells for.

The brands and numbers are made up for teaching. Real positioning maps come from surveys of what customers think, not from what the company says about itself.

## Predict first

Two prediction cards sit above the challenges. Students pick Goes up, Goes down, or Stays the same; the tool makes the change on screen and shows before and after.

- **From the biggest group to an empty one** (pizza, Families bubble at 4, 4.5 to the "Good pizza, fair price" bubble at 4.5, 8): customers **go up**, about 236 to 350. On the Families bubble you split 400 customers with Planet Pie, who already has regulars. Up top, nobody else is close, so you get the whole group. Follow-up: why might that spot be empty? (Good pizza at a fair price is hard to make cheaply.)
- **Even healthier bar** (snack bars, 8, 8 to 8, 10): customers **go down**, about 127 to 13. Gym regulars sit at 8, 8; moving away from them hands them to Peak Protein. Follow-up: "better" product, fewer customers. Why? (Customers want what fits them, not the most of everything.)

## Challenges

Three challenge cards sit under the tool. Each Start button loads its scenario and makes a link you can post. Sample solutions (there are others):

- **Find the gap** (pizza, no stretch): price 4.5, quality 8 wins all 350 of "Good pizza, fair price." Price 4.5, quality 5 wins about 429 by picking up some families too. Ask which spot is easier to defend.
- **Premium shoes** (running shoes, price 7 or more): price 7.5, performance 8 wins about 411. It sits between the new runners and the racers, so it picks up customers from both. Compare with joining the crowd at 8.5, 8.5.
- **Win the gym crowd** (snack bars, price 7 or more and healthiness 7 or more): price 8, healthiness 8 wins about 127. It is the only spot that works; half a step away drops to about 60. Debrief: why is a niche so sensitive to small moves?

## Common misconceptions

- **"Go where the most customers are."** The biggest group is usually the most crowded. Winning a slice of 400 can be worse than owning all of 300.
- **"Any empty spot is an opportunity."** Some spots are empty because nobody wants that combination. Others are empty because nobody can make it at a profit.
- **"Copy the most successful brand."** Established brands keep their regulars. A newcomer sitting in the same spot splits the group and usually gets the smaller share.
- **"Positioning is about the product."** It's about how customers see the product compared to the alternatives. That's why the map has rivals and customers on it, not just your brand.
