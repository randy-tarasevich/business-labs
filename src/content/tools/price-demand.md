## How I use it

**Marketing, pricing unit.** 15–20 minutes. Pairs well with Break-Even Lab the day before.

1. **Predict:** Load the concert with "Show the best prices" unchecked. Each student writes the ticket price they think earns the most revenue, and the one that earns the most profit.
2. **Test:** Students slide the price to find both, then check the box to confirm. Then they work the "Try it" questions.
3. **Explain:** One sentence each: why does the profit-maximizing price sit above the revenue-maximizing price?

Demand is a straight line built from three facts students can reason about: a price you've tested, how many sold at that price, and how many customers you lose per $1 increase. For an entrepreneurship project, students can plug in survey results from their own product idea.

## Answer notes

| Preset | Starting price | Elasticity there | Best revenue | Best profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Concert tickets | $40 | 0.80 (inelastic) | $45.00 → $60,750 | $47.50 → $34,188 |
| School hoodies | $35 | 1.17 (elastic) | $32.50 → $4,225 | $41.50 → $2,009 |
| Food truck tacos | $4 | 0.80 (inelastic) | $4.50 → $2,430 | $5.25 → $888 |
| Movie popcorn | $8 | 0.30 (inelastic) | $17.33 → $4,507 | $17.83 → $4,250 |

With straight-line demand, revenue always peaks at half the price where nobody buys, and that's exactly where elasticity equals 1. The best price for profit is halfway between that "nobody buys" price and the variable cost, so a $4 cost increase moves it up $2.

## Predict first

Two prediction cards sit above the challenges. Students pick Goes up, Goes down, or Stays the same; the tool makes the change on screen and shows before and after.

- **Higher rent** (concert, fixed costs $20,000 to $25,000): the most profitable price **stays the same** at $47.50. Fixed costs are paid no matter what price you pick, so they lower profit but don't move the best price. Follow-up: so should the venue raise prices to cover rent? (Only if it was already priced too low.)
- **Raise popcorn to $10** (movie popcorn, $8 to $10): revenue **goes up**, $3,200 to $3,700. Buckets drop from 400 to 370, but each one brings in $2 more. Popcorn demand is inelastic here. Follow-up: where would raising the price start to hurt? (Above about $17.33, the revenue peak.)

## Challenges

Three challenge cards sit under the tool. Each Start button loads its scenario and makes a link you can post. Sample solutions (there are others):

- **Most ticket revenue** (concert, demand locked): $44 to $46 all work; $45 gives $60,750, the peak. Revenue tops out at half the price where nobody buys ($90 ÷ 2).
- **Most hoodie profit** (hoodies, demand and costs locked): $40 to $43 all work; $41.50 gives about $2,009. Note that the best price for profit ($41.50) is higher than the best price for revenue ($32.50): each hoodie costs $18 to make, so selling fewer at a higher price wins.
- **Popcorn costs went up** (variable cost $3, demand locked): anything from $15 to $23 works; $18 sells 250 buckets for $3,750 profit, the most is about $3,760 near $18.83. Debrief: the theater was charging far too little before costs went up.

## Common misconceptions

- Students think the highest price earns the most. The curve shows revenue rising, peaking, and falling.
- Students think most revenue and most profit are the same goal. Profit subtracts costs, so it peaks at a higher price.
- Elastic vs. inelastic gets memorized backward. Anchor it: elastic stretches a lot (customers react a lot).
- Elasticity changes along the curve. The same product can be inelastic at a low price and elastic at a high one.
