## How I use it

**Level: Advanced Accounting.** 20–25 minutes, after students can read a balance sheet and income statement.

1. **Predict:** Show the four business names only. Students rank them from healthiest to riskiest before seeing any numbers.
2. **Test:** Load each preset and compare the ratios to their ranking. Pairs work the "Try it" questions.
3. **Explain:** Role-play: one student is the owner of the furniture store asking for a loan, the other is the banker. The banker has to cite two ratios.

Students can type in numbers from any statement, including a real company's annual report, so it works for a stock or company research project.

The colored zones are rough rules of thumb. Stress that "healthy" depends on the industry. Inventory turnover uses ending inventory (not the average) to keep the inputs simple.

## Answer notes

| Ratio | Hardware | Grocery | Software | Furniture |

| Current | 2.50 | 1.50 | 3.60 | 0.87 |
| Quick | 1.13 | 0.58 | 3.60 | 0.17 |
| Debt-to-equity | 0.60 | 1.36 | 0.38 | 6.50 |
| Gross margin | 35% | 25% | 80% | 30% |
| Net margin | 7.5% | 2.0% | −13.3% | −3.9% |
| Return on assets | 16.0% | 9.2% | −18.2% | −5.8% |
| Return on equity | 25.5% | 21.8% | −25.0% | −43.8% |
| Inventory turnover | 4.7× | 16.4× | n/a | 3.0× |

The grocery store is the teaching case: weak-looking margins, but high turnover and a strong return on equity.

## Common misconceptions

- Students think a higher ratio is always better. A very high current ratio can mean cash sitting idle; a high debt-to-equity is risky.
- Students compare ratios across industries. A 2% net margin is normal for groceries and alarming for software.
- Students confuse gross margin and net margin. Gross margin only subtracts the cost of products; net margin subtracts every expense.
- Return on equity can look great when equity is tiny. Pair it with debt-to-equity.
