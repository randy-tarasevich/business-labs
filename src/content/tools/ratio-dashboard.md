## How I use it

**Level: Advanced Accounting.** 20–25 minutes, after students can read a balance sheet and income statement.

1. **Predict:** Show the four business names only. Students rank them from healthiest to riskiest before seeing any numbers.
2. **Test:** Load each preset and compare the ratios to their ranking. Pairs work the "Try it" questions.
3. **Explain:** Role-play: one student is the owner of the furniture store asking for a loan, the other is the banker. The banker has to cite two ratios.

Students can type in numbers from any statement, including a real company's annual report, so it works for a stock or company research project.

The colored zones are rough rules of thumb. Stress that "healthy" depends on the industry. Inventory turnover uses ending inventory (not the average) to keep the inputs simple.

The grocery store is the teaching case: weak-looking margins, but high turnover and a strong return on equity.

## Predict first

Two prediction cards sit above the challenges. Students pick Goes up, Goes down, or Stays the same; the tool makes the change on screen and shows before and after.

- **Sales and cost of goods sold both double** (hardware store): gross margin **stays the same** at 35%. A ratio compares two numbers, so doubling both changes nothing. Follow-up: which ratios would change? (Net margin, return on assets, and turnover, because net income, assets, and inventory didn't double.)
- **Pay $60,000 of bills with cash** (grocery store, current assets $120,000, current liabilities $60,000): the current ratio **goes up**, 1.50 to 2.00. Spending cash usually feels like it hurts, but paying a bill shrinks the bottom of the fraction faster. Follow-up: what if the ratio started below 1? (Paying bills would make it worse.)

## Challenges

Three challenge cards sit under the tool. Each Start button loads its scenario and makes a link you can post. Sample solutions (there are others):

- **Pay this year's bills** (furniture store, current assets and total liabilities locked): current liabilities have to drop to about $173,333 or less ($170,000 works). The debt doesn't disappear; it moves to a long-term loan. Ask why a bank might agree to that.
- **Less on the shelves** (grocery store, current assets and liabilities locked): inventory has to fall to $60,000 or less. That's a big clearance. Debrief: why does a grocery store run a low quick ratio and still pay its bills? (Inventory sells fast.)
- **Owe less than twice** (furniture store, total assets stay $600,000): total liabilities under $400,000 ($390,000 works, about 1.86). Owners put in $130,000 or more to pay down debt.

## Common misconceptions

- Students think a higher ratio is always better. A very high current ratio can mean cash sitting idle; a high debt-to-equity is risky.
- Students compare ratios across industries. A 2% net margin is normal for groceries and alarming for software.
- Students confuse gross margin and net margin. Gross margin only subtracts the cost of products; net margin subtracts every expense.
- Return on equity can look great when equity is tiny. Pair it with debt-to-equity.
