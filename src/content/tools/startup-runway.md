## How I use it

A 15–20 minute **predict, test, explain** activity. Works well right after Break-Even Lab: break-even says how many sales you need, runway says how long you have to get there.

1. **Predict:** Load the food truck and cover the chart. Students read the costs and guess the month the cash runs out.
2. **Test:** Reveal the chart, then pairs work the "Try it" questions. For question 1, each pair takes a different lever (cash, truck price, monthly costs, sales, growth) and reports how big a change it took.
3. **Explain:** Each pair writes one sentence on why a business can be "profitable soon" and still fail.

For business plan projects, students replace the preset line items with their own estimates. Every line item can be edited. Nothing is saved, so have them screenshot the chart for their plan.

## Answer notes

- **Food truck:** runs out in month 7. Cash bottoms out at about −$2,990 in month 10, and month 11 is the first month in the black. Any of these gets it through month 24: about $3,000 more starting cash, a truck about $3,000 cheaper, cutting part-time help by $500 a month, $500 more in month 1 sales, or 10% growth instead of 8%. The point: the business works, it just needs a cushion.
- **Growth at 0%:** the food truck loses $3,100 every month and runs out in month 5. The bars drop in a straight line because every month is identical.
- **Phone case startup:** runs out in month 3 and falls about $10,860 below zero before it turns around. This one needs real funding, not a small tweak.
- **$2,000 once vs. $200 a month (phone case):** almost a tie at the low point (about −$8,860 vs. −$8,840). After month 10 the monthly saving pulls ahead, and by month 24 it's $2,800 better. One-time savings help early; recurring savings win over time.
- **T-shirt shop:** safe the whole way. Cash never drops below about $2,500.
- **Dog walking:** in the black from month 1, so runway never matters. Good contrast: low startup cost businesses are easier to survive.

## Predict first

Two prediction cards sit above the challenges. Students pick Goes up, Goes down, or Stays the same; the tool makes the change on screen and shows before and after.

- **More starting cash** (food truck, $75,000 to $95,000): the first profitable month **stays the same**, month 11. Cash in the bank doesn't change sales or costs; it only decides whether you survive until profit arrives. Follow-up: what did change? (The truck never runs out; the lowest balance goes from about −$2,989 to about $17,011.)
- **Cut the ads in half** (phone case startup, $2,000 to $1,000 a month, sales unchanged): the lowest cash balance **goes up**, about −$10,862 to −$1,556. It still runs out (month 5 instead of 3). Follow-up: is "sales unchanged" realistic when you cut ads? (Probably not, which is why this decision is hard.)

## Challenges

Three challenge cards sit under the tool. Each Start button loads its scenario and makes a link you can post. Sample solutions (there are others):

- **Raise enough cash** (food truck, costs and sales plan locked): $78,000 or more. At $78,000 the lowest balance is about $11 in month 10, which is cutting it close. Ask: how much extra would you raise for safety?
- **No new money** (phone case startup, cash, month 1 sales, and growth locked): cut online ads to $800 a month or less (lowest balance about $14). Cutting variable costs or other fixed costs also works. Debrief: which cut hurts the business least?
- **Keep a cushion** (food truck, cash and sales plan locked): buy a cheaper truck, $32,000 or less (a $30,000 truck gives a low of about $12,011). Cutting part-time help to $1,500 a month gives about $7,976, not enough on its own. Startup costs matter because they come out before day one.

## Common misconceptions

- Students treat "profitable" and "has cash" as the same thing. The food truck is profitable by month 11 and still below zero from month 7 through month 13. In real life, it would have closed in month 7.
- Students forget startup costs come out before opening day. Point to the "Cash on opening day" number.
- Students assume growth will save them. Push back: what if growth is 4% instead of 8%? Which inputs can the owner actually control?
- The simple runway formula assumes sales never grow, so it underestimates runway for growing businesses. The chart plays out each month instead.
