## How I use it

**Intro to Business, economics unit.** 20–25 minutes. Comes right after Comparative Advantage and right before Price and Demand Lab.

1. **Predict:** Project the strawberries with nothing clicked. Read one event out loud ("Drought hits local farms"). Students write three things: which curve moves (supply or demand), which way (left or right), and what happens to price and quantity.
2. **Test:** Click the event and check. Repeat with two or three more events, then let pairs work the "Try it" questions.
3. **Explain:** Each pair writes one sentence on the difference between "supply decreased" and "quantity supplied decreased."

The events are the heart of the tool. Every one changes something *other than* the price of the product, so every one shifts a curve. When students start saying "the price went up, so demand went down," send them back to the chart: the demand curve didn't move.

Locking the price is a quick way into price ceilings and floors. A locked price below the market price is a cap (shortage). Above it is a floor (surplus).

- **Two shifts at once (Try it #2):** drought plus sleep study gives $5.27 and 1,109 quarts. When both shifts push price the same way, price is certain and quantity depends on which shift is bigger. With the sleep study on, a supply shift of −800 is the tipping point: quantity lands back at exactly 1,000.
- **Price cap (Try it #4):** at $3.00, buyers want 1,150 and sellers offer 600, a shortage of 550.
- **Gas vs. strawberries (Try it #5):** gas demand is much steeper (buyers lose only 1,000 gallons per $1 out of 20,000), so a supply cut mostly shows up as a higher price. This sets up elasticity in Price and Demand Lab.

The four-shift pattern students should be able to state without the tool:

| Change | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | Up | Up |
| Demand decreases | Down | Down |
| Supply increases | Down | Up |
| Supply decreases | Up | Down |

## Common misconceptions

- **"Price went up, so demand went down."** A price change moves buyers *along* the demand curve (quantity demanded). Only a change in something else (tastes, income, the price of a substitute, the season) shifts demand.
- **"Supply went up because the price went up."** Same mix-up on the seller side. In Try it #3 the athlete shifts demand; sellers then offer more pairs at the higher price, but the supply curve never moves.
- **Left and right get mixed up with up and down.** A supply decrease shifts the curve left, which *looks* like it moved up. Anchor on the quantity axis: left means fewer at every price.
- **Cheaper materials shift demand.** Costs are a seller-side change. Lower costs let sellers offer more at every price, so supply shifts right.
- **A price cap helps everyone.** Buyers who get the product pay less, but fewer units get sold than at the market price, and some buyers go without.
