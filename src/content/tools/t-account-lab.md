## How I use it

**Accounting 1, right after the Accounting Equation Lab.** 25–35 minutes. It uses the same 12 transactions, so students already know what each one does to the equation. Now they learn to write it the way accountants do.

1. **Predict:** Before each entry, students say out loud (or on a whiteboard) which account goes up or down and whether that's a debit or a credit, using the rules table.
2. **Test:** Enter the debit and credit, then Check. A wrong entry gets a hint that names the account type and which way it should move, and names the account on the second miss. Swapped sides get their own message.
3. **Explain:** After the last entry, pairs work the "Try it" questions. Question 4 ties this tool back to the equation.

Point students to the T-accounts after every few entries. Cash fills up fast on both sides; it's a good moment to show that the balance goes on the bigger side.

The tool splits owner's equity into Capital, Drawing, Revenue, and Expenses, the way most Accounting 1 textbooks do. The Accounting Equation Lab lumps them into one Owner's Equity line. Try it #4 shows they match.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Debit means increase, credit means decrease."** It depends on the account type. Debit is just the left side. Cash goes up with a debit; Loan Payable goes up with a credit.
- **"Credit is good, debit is bad."** That comes from bank statements, where the bank is looking at your account from *its* side. In the business's own books, a credit to Cash means cash went down.
- **"Paying a bill is an expense."** Transaction 8 pays off something already recorded in transaction 4. The debit goes to Accounts Payable, not an expense account.
- **"Owner withdrawals are expenses."** Drawing reduces equity, but it isn't a cost of running the business. It gets its own account.
- **"If the trial balance balances, the books are right."** It only proves debits equal credits. A wrong account on the right side, or a missing entry, still balances.
